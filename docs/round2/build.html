Section 2

02Try it: the core workflow

The demo signs you in by picking a person, so judges can try both sides without creating accounts; signing up for your own empty workspace is also possible. A guided tour runs on the first visit to each side and can be replayed from Settings.

  1. Sign in as Dev Sharma (creator). Open My reviews, paste a caption such as "Kitepath has 2 million learners. Guaranteed results!", optionally attach an image, PDF or deck, and run the review. A video also needs a short brief saying what and who is in it, typed or as a voice note.
  2. Read the findings. The 2 million claim is checked against the approved fact (1.2 million) and flagged with the fact cited; "Guaranteed results" is flagged as a promise that needs proof. Each issue is marked in place, with a confidence and a suggested fix.
  3. Fix or push back. Choose the fixes to use and press Apply and re-check. If a reading is wrong, dispute it with an explanation, or ask the brand team. Then send the piece for approval.
  4. Switch account to Arjun Mehta (brand team). The piece is in the review queue, ranked by what needs attention first. Walk through its issues one by one: ask for the fix, keep it with a reason, or escalate.
  5. Decide. Approve, or send it back with the fixes asked for. Every override needs a written reason, and every step goes into the audit log.
Section 3

03Completed features

Everything below is finished and works on the live link. Items that are designed or only partly built are listed in Section 11, not here.

Reads everything in a post

  • Caption and text inside images (OCR)
  • Every page of a PDF or Word file (up to 40), including text drawn as shapes and scanned pages
  • Every slide of a deck (up to 60), with speaker notes and hidden slides; every sheet of a workbook
  • HTML pages and emails: title, share text, alt text; nothing linked is fetched
  • Video: on-screen text, subtitles and speech, each placed in time
  • Speech in English, Hindi and Hinglish, transcribed on our server

Checks against the brand's own sources

  • Claims against approved facts with validity dates: contradicted, unsupported or outdated, with the fact cited
  • Promotional and legal claims that need proof: guarantees, "#1", superlatives
  • Banned and preferred words, and confidential terms, including look-alike spellings and codenames said aloud
  • Tone, messaging and topic drift, judged against the brand's rules and examples
  • Official artwork: stretched, squashed, skewed, mirrored, recoloured or redrawn, boxed on the image
  • Redrawn mascots, naming what changed (a wrong colour, an extra eye, a missing tail)
  • Faces against the brand's approved people (the list is kept through the API for now)
  • Accessibility: contrast over images, alt text, readability, hashtag casing
  • Cross-checks: caption, image text, voice-over and on-screen text must agree

Explains, fixes and keeps people in charge

  • Every issue marked in place, with the rule or fact behind it and a confidence
  • Document preview with a box on each flagged phrase; sheets as tables with flagged cells tinted
  • Fixes that swap, rewrite or remove, checked so the offer and call to action survive; applied in one click
  • Disputes read on the re-check; escalation to the brand team, which can be withdrawn
  • Versions for every resubmission, comparable side by side
  • A check that could not run is shown as "not checked", never as passed

Brand Console

  • Dashboard, risk-ranked review queue, escalations inbox; walk through issues one by one, then approve or send back, with a written reason for every override
  • Library of rules, dated facts, terms, confidential terms, artwork and public contacts (an unlisted phone number in a post is flagged)
  • Brand events (a launch, sale or crisis) whose guidance applies while they run
  • Sign up as a manager for your own workspace, with a join code for creators
  • Intake: brand books, price sheets, decks and logos become draft rules and facts that a person approves
  • Guideline health (rules people keep overriding), creator drift profiles
  • Confidence calibrated from reviewer decisions; warning thresholds learned from them

Easy to pick up, safe by default

  • Guided tour per side, plain-word summaries, every status and score explained where it appears
  • Ask Verytas, a chat over the brand's own data; a page on how Verytas uses AI
  • Redaction before any model call; signed findings; hash-chained audit log; role checks on every request; light and dark mode
Section 4

04Architecture and key decisions

Two web apps share one codebase and one API. Prism, the review engine, splits each submission into parts and runs a check agent on each. Rules run in code first; models only judge meaning, and only after confidential terms are removed.

Main technical decisions

DecisionWhy
Rules before modelsFacts, words, confidential terms, artwork and contrast are checked in code: free, instant and fully explainable. Models judge only meaning (tone, implied claims, sarcasm), and a finding from a model alone can never block a post.
Never silently passEvery check reports its status. A timeout, an unreadable language or a model outage shows as "not checked" or "degraded" on the review, never as clean.
Heavy media stays localOCR, speech to text, PDF rendering and artwork matching run on our server's CPU, so raw images and audio never leave it. Only redacted text and pixels reach a model.
Vision on a worker that sleepsFinding redrawn mascots needs three image models, too heavy for the always-on server. They run on a second instance that the API starts when needed and that stops itself after 20 idle minutes, so it costs nothing most of the time.
Pull-based deploysCI moves a live branch only when every check passes; the server pulls it with a read-only key, builds beside the running version, health-checks it and rolls back on failure. Nothing needs inbound access from CI.
Untrusted files in a sealed roomWord and PowerPoint previews need LibreOffice, a large program reading files strangers upload. It runs one file at a time, as a throwaway user, with no network and no view of the app's files, under memory and time limits.

How the pieces talk

Prism to models

Every model call goes through the redactor and a governor with retries, a shared pool of model slots, per-step deadlines and a circuit breaker per model, so a burst of uploads queues instead of failing. Two Ollama keys are held; a refused or exhausted key hands over to the second for ten minutes.

Decisions back to learning

Each reviewer decision updates the rule's precision, the calibration curve and the warning floor. Every change to a learned value is written to the audit log with its before and after.

Section 6

06What was hard, and how we solved it

Five problems took most of the engineering. Each solution was measured before we kept it.

1. Claims we can trust

Text is split into single claims, and each is matched to approved facts that are valid on the publish date. Numbers, units and dates are compared in code; a model only decides whether a claim and a fact are about the same thing. A claim backed only by an expired fact is outdated, so "500+ teams" is caught after the approved figure became "800+ teams". Each verdict cites the fact it used.

2. Fixes that keep what the creator meant

Our first gate asked one question: is the message, call to action and audience the same? It blocked the very fixes some rules exist for, such as removing a dig at a competitor. We built an evaluation (73 findings, two graders that never grade their own output) and rebuilt the step: the model now picks an operation per finding (replace the words, rewrite the sentence, or delete a sentence that only mocks or pressures), sees the brand's own good and bad examples, and the gate asks narrow questions about the rule. A figure the creator never wrote is dropped by code. Useful fixes went from 45% to 82%, with no invented numbers.

3. Seeing redrawn artwork

Official logos are found by keypoints or template matching, then their placement is refined and read as stretch, skew or flip. Our first comparison checked the image against the artwork; comparing in the other direction, after warping the artwork into the image's pixels, cut false alarms from 12.6% to 4.7%. AI-redrawn mascots need open-vocabulary detection, segmentation and image embeddings, so they run on the sleeping worker, and Verytas names what changed rather than just "does not match".

4. Hindi and Hinglish speech

The general speech model mishears much spoken Hindi. Rather than trust the transcript, confidential codenames are also matched by how they sound, and spoken numbers ("chalis pratishat") are read as figures, so a voice-over that says a price different from the caption is still caught.

5. Confidence that means something

Self-reported model confidence runs high. Our judge answers with a certainty band; borderline answers are asked twice more and averaged, and once enough decisions exist, confidence is mapped to how often reviewers actually agree (isotonic regression). We also tested a cascade that re-asked the large model only on close calls; it never fired, because the small model's mistakes came back at full certainty. Asking both models on must-fix rules was right on 30 of 32 sentences against 27.

Also hard: keeping the assistants' instructions private. Asking Ask Verytas for its prompt gets a fixed answer, and a reply that starts reciting its instructions is cut off before any of it is sent. Seven live attacks were refused on the deployed site.

Testing and reliability

  • Over 630 backend tests run offline with no keys on every push, alongside web type checks, lint and a production build.
  • A black-box simulator (blackbox/) knows Verytas only through its public API. It invents brands and posts with planted violations, scores what comes back, and steers each round toward what Verytas misses.
  • Every release is health-checked before it takes traffic, and rolled back automatically if it fails.
  • A first security review ran on the codebase and on the live site; confirmed findings are fixed, each with a regression test. Six areas it did not cover are listed for the next pass in docs/security/README.md.

Section 9

09Security, privacy and responsible AI

A tool that reads a brand's unreleased plans and judges people's work has to be safe with both.

Confidential terms never reach a model

They are replaced by placeholders in text and blacked out in image pixels first, including a codename written without its "Project". Confidential-tier content is never sent as pixels at all.

Tamper-evident record

Every finding is signed with Ed25519 when it is made, and every action is written to a SHA-256 hash-chained audit log that anyone with audit access can verify.

People decide

Fixes are never applied without a person choosing them. A model-only finding cannot block a post. Overrides need a written reason. Creators can dispute any reading.

Hostile input expected

File types are read from the bytes; archives and images are read within budgets; content under review is treated as data, never as instructions; bribes and "WhatsApp me" style contact swaps are refused before they are stored.

Faces follow the brand's records

A face is identified only against the brand's approved people. A public portrait is looked up only when the brief names someone and the content is not confidential.

Open about AI

A page in the app lists which models run, what each one does, and their measured accuracy, including the weak spots. Each review lists the checks that ran and the models used.

Before the repository went public

The whole git history was scanned for secrets with gitleaks (none found). Keys live only in server files readable by the service. Dependencies install from hashed lock files, most model files are pinned by SHA-256 (the speech model by revision only, for now), and CI actions are pinned to commit SHAs.

Use of AI tools in building Verytas

We used Claude Code (Anthropic) as a coding assistant throughout development, for writing and reviewing code, tests and documentation. The team set the design, made every product and technical decision, reviewed what was produced, and tested the result. Inside the product, the models listed in Section 5 do the checking, and the app says so.

Section 11

11What does not work yet

Things a judge may run into, stated plainly.

AreaWhat to expect
Recoloured app iconsAn icon redrawn in other colours is caught about a third of the time. Stretched, mirrored, skewed and redrawn artwork is caught far more reliably.
Hindi speechThe speech model mishears much spoken Hindi. Codenames are still caught by sound and spoken figures are read, but a long Hindi voice-over is transcribed roughly.
Mascots on busy backgroundsThe cut-out takes in some background, so on busy backgrounds a correct mascot is wrongly flagged in about half of test scenes (53% to 63%, against 13% on plain ones). The vision model's opinion alone never blocks a post.
First mascot check after a quiet spellThe vision worker sleeps after 20 idle minutes, so the next review that needs it waits while it starts (up to about four minutes). If it does not start in time, that check is shown as not run.
SpeedModels run on a remote service without our own GPU. Text takes a few seconds; images, documents and video take longer. If the service is down or out of quota, those checks show as degraded, not passed.
Size limitsDocuments are read up to 40 pages and decks up to 60 slides; the review says when the rest was not checked. Uploads are capped (images 6 MB, documents 20 MB, video 150 MB and 15 minutes).
One-on-one meetingsShown in both apps and marked "Planned, not in this demo". Designed, not built.
Console screensApproved people, marking moments in a video and findings on a video timeline are supported by the API but have no screens yet.
Shared demo accountsEvery judge signs in as the same sample people, so you may see pieces other visitors submitted. The demo data resets every two weeks.
Your own workspace starts emptySigning up as a manager creates a new company with no rules, facts or artwork, so reviews find little until some are added (or a brand book is handed to Intake). Sign-ups and reviews per new workspace are capped per day to protect the model budget. The sample companies show everything working.
IntegrationsThe MCP server, Slack notifications, CMS hook and performance feedback from the original plan are not built. Brand events change rules but not yet facts, so a price change is still entered as a new fact.
Section 12

12Three improvements for Round 3

Each is new capability, sized for what two people can ship and measure in Round 3.

1

One-on-one meetings between a creator and their manager

What it is. A creator asks the manager who looks after them for a meeting by chat, voice or video, from a review or from their profile. The manager accepts, or proposes another time or way. Voice calls go through temporary masked numbers, the way delivery apps connect a rider and a customer, so neither side sees the other's real number. Only the time, the way and an outcome note go on the record, linked to the pieces discussed.

What it changes. A disagreement that today goes back and forth in comments ("is this claim allowed?") is settled in one conversation, with the decision recorded against the review. Creators get a person, not only a queue, and phone numbers stay private.

2

Sharper vision and speech, on our own GPU

What it is. Upgrades to the engines that are weakest today. Recoloured icons: a comparison that separates an icon's shape from its colours, so a correct icon in the wrong colours is caught. Hindi speech: a model trained for Hindi and Hinglish in place of the general one. A GPU server that wakes on demand to run the language and vision models ourselves, with the remote service as a fallback.

What it changes. Fewer missed issues and fewer false alarms on images and Hindi video, faster reviews, and brand content that no longer has to leave our servers to be checked. Each change is measured on the same benchmarks as today, and we publish the before and after.

3

Managing it all from the Brand Console

What it is. Screens for work that today needs the API. An approved-people tab in the Library, where the brand team adds the people allowed to appear in content, with their agreement dates. A video player that shows every finding as a mark on its timeline and jumps to it. Tools for managers to mark a moment in a video or an area of an image and comment on it, which the creator sees in place.

What it changes. The brand team runs governance end to end without technical help. Reviewing a video becomes as direct as reviewing a caption, and feedback points at the exact second or region instead of describing it in words.
